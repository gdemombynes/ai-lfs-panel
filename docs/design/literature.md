# Related work (as of 2026-09-06)

Closest to this project, found through Google Scholar; PDFs saved in the
session scratchpad, not in the repo.

## Labour-market effects in developing countries

- **Ravanilla (2026), "Generative AI and Labor Displacement in the Global
  South: Evidence from Philippine Business Process Outsourcing"** (SSRN
  7313383; also "Early Signs of the Generative AI Labor Market Shock in the
  Global South"). Philippine LFS microdata by province of workplace, exposure
  = pre-ChatGPT occupational composition x observed usage from the Anthropic
  Economic Index, outcomes traced as US client firms adopt AI. Employment
  growth slows in the most exposed provinces, industries and municipalities,
  about 250,000 foregone jobs by mid-2025; adjustment through reduced hiring,
  not layoffs; wages and hours unchanged; displaced workers enter
  unemployment and first-time overseas departures fall.
- **Aguirre and Meloni (2026), "Early Labor-Market Responses to Generative
  AI in a Developing Country: Evidence from Brazil"** (SSRN 6902947). PNAD
  Contínua 2017Q1-2024Q4 plus CAGED hiring and separation records; exposure
  = Eloundou et al. task scores mapped to Brazilian occupations, split at the
  employment-weighted median; difference in differences around November
  2022. Workers aged 16-21 in high-exposure occupations earn 4.7 % less;
  1.5 pp less likely to be formally employed; administrative hiring wages for
  young hires fall 1.3 % more. No effect for prime-age or older workers.
  Adjustment runs through entry wages and contract terms rather than
  aggregate hiring levels.
- **Huang, Liu, Wang and Yu (2026), "Who Takes the Hit? The Uneven Impacts
  of Generative AI on Labor Demand Across Countries"** (SSRN). Cross-country
  design on ILOSTAT occupation-level employment.
- **Demombynes, Langbein and Weber (2025), "The exposure of workers to
  artificial intelligence in low- and middle-income countries"** (World
  Bank). Exposure with microdata across LMICs.
- **Gmyrek, Winkler and Garganta (2024), "Buffer or bottleneck?"** ILO
  exposure applied to Latin American microdata with a digital-access
  adjustment.
- **IMF WP 25/43 (2025)** on the Philippines: exposure and complementarity.

## Call centres specifically

- **Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (QJE 2025), "Generative AI at Work".**
  5,179 support agents, most in the Philippines; productivity +14 % on
  average, +34 % for novices; retention improves. No headcount results.
- **Doellgast, O'Brady, Kim and Walters (2023), "AI in contact centers"**
  (Cornell ILR). Worker survey and interviews on algorithmic management.
- **Zhang, Li and Liu (2023)**, event study of a conversational agent in a
  Chinese telecom call centre: operational performance only.
- **Whitmore (2025)**, contact-centre job hierarchies 2018-2024: claims an
  "hourglass" pattern (entry and expert roles grow, middle hollows).

## High-income benchmarks

- Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen (2025), "Canaries in the Coal Mine": US
  payroll, ages 22-25 in exposed occupations, employment down 6-8 %;
  customer-service representatives among the affected occupations.
- Klein Teeselink (2025), UK analogue. Hosseini Maasoum and Lichtinger
  (2025), LinkedIn firm-level: junior employment falls 7.7-12 % in adopting
  firms through reduced hiring.
- Humlum and Vestergaard (2025), Denmark: no earnings or hours effects.

## World Development Report 2026 and its background papers

WDR 2026, "The Promise of Artificial Intelligence" (World Bank, August
2026; full report and overview PDFs saved in the session scratchpad).
Chapter 4 covers economic impacts. What it says on jobs:

- AI is "not yet replacing large numbers of jobs in most developing
  countries", but business-process outsourcing (call centres, back-office
  services) faces "growing pressure". The chapter opens with a Philippine
  call-centre agent using an AI assist tool; it cites the BPO sector at about
  1.3 million workers in the Philippines.
- All quantitative post-2022 evidence in the report comes from online job
  postings, not household surveys: Huang et al. (below) across 84 countries;
  a China posting study (Liu, Wang et al. 2025) with entry-level effects; a
  South Asia posting study (World Bank 2025, box 4.3) with monthly listings
  down about 20 % for the most exposed white-collar occupations and hiring
  down 1.6 % overall, larger for globally connected firms. Entry-level
  effects are quoted from Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen and from Hosseini
  and Lichtinger, all high-income. No labour-force-survey employment
  evidence for LMICs appears anywhere in the report.
- Policy line: prepare call-centre and BPO workers to move into tasks that
  complement AI; social-protection triggers for displaced call-centre
  agents.

Background papers (list at worldbank.org/en/publication/wdr2026/brief/
world-development-report-2026-background-papers). The two by Hernán
Winkler:

- **Gmyrek, Viollaz and Winkler (2026), "Disruption without Dividend? How
  the Digital Divide and Task Differences Split GenAI's Global Impact"**
  (Policy Research WP 11328, March 2026; also ILO WP 166; reproducibility
  package reproducibility.worldbank.org/catalog/449). Cross-sectional
  exposure, not a post-2022 outcome study. Microdata for 50 countries
  (LABLAC/SEDLAC, WAEMU, PIAAC round 2, GLD) with ISCO-08 at up to 4 digits,
  extended to 135 countries with ILOSTAT 2-digit tabulations and kNN donor
  imputation; internet access modelled by logit and used to discount
  augmentation exposure; PIAAC and STEP task measures to test whether
  exposed occupations do the same tasks across income levels. Uses the same
  ILO 2025 index (Gmyrek et al. WP 140) and the same GLD collection as this
  project. Findings: exposure rises with GDP per capita (about 32 % of
  employment in high-income, 15 % in low-income), driven by automation-side
  occupations (clerical, gradients 3-4); augmentation potential is spread
  evenly; automation-exposed workers have internet access even in poor
  countries while augmentation-exposed workers often do not ("small buffer,
  big bottlenecks"); within countries, automation exposure concentrates in
  women and workers aged 16-35 (10.4 % of 16-35 year olds vs 4.3 % of
  56-65 year olds in upper-middle-income countries); workers in developing
  countries perform far fewer non-routine analytical tasks within the same
  exposed occupations, so uniform indices overstate exposure there. The
  paper names the "white-collar bypass": entry-level office jobs that were
  stepping-stones for young workers and for women may never emerge. Sample
  overlap with this panel: Argentina (SEDLAC 2023, 2-digit), Brazil (SEDLAC
  2023, 2-digit), Ecuador (SEDLAC 2023, 4-digit), Georgia (GLD 2022),
  Mexico (GLD 2023), Peru (SEDLAC 2023), India (GLD 2022, 3-digit); no
  Colombia, South Africa, Philippines or Nigeria.
- **Gmyrek, Segura, Winkler and Yu (2026), "Generative AI and the Global
  Redistribution of Expertise"** (WDR background paper, version 29 July
  2026; cited in the report as "Expertise and Automation by GenAI"). Builds
  an expertise score for the 3,265 ISCO-08 task statements (word-list
  index, 90-task manual review, Gemini-anchored rating) and crosses it with
  the ILO task-level exposure scores, then merges with 26 four-digit
  countries from the same harmonized collection (1.42 million workers aged
  16-65; includes Ecuador, Georgia, Mexico, Peru). Findings: among
  professionals and technicians the automatable tasks are the inexpert
  ones (documentation, correspondence, scheduling), so automation raises
  the expertise of remaining work and the entry barrier; among clerical
  support workers the expert tasks are the exposed ones, so the barrier
  falls; the affected employment is concentrated in high-income countries.
  Framework is Autor and Thompson (2025) "Expertise". Relevant here because
  it predicts opposite entry-margin effects for clerical cells (ISCO 4,
  including 4222 contact-centre clerks) and for professional cells.

Other WDR background papers relevant to this project:

- **Huang, Liu, Wang and Yu (2026), "Who Takes the Hit?"** (already listed
  above; the WDR's main cross-country evidence). 555 million Lightcast
  postings, 84 countries, 2021Q1-2025Q2, DiD and event study around
  November 2022, above- vs below-median substitution vulnerability.
  High-income countries: postings in vulnerable occupations down 5.8 %,
  growing over time. Middle- and low-income: small and insignificant;
  effect rises with GDP per capita and with AI adoption, complementary
  factors and digitally tradable services. Coverage of our countries in
  Lightcast is thin (Philippines 87 thousand postings, India 65 thousand),
  which is the gap an LFS panel fills.
- **Kruse and Yu (2026), "The AI Exposure of Export-Linked Jobs"**: LFS
  plus input-output tables for 26 economies; export-linked jobs are more
  substitution-exposed in richer economies.

## Where this project sits

Neither developing-country paper is cross-country, and neither looks at
call-centre workers as an occupation across surveys. The WDR 2026 has no
LFS-based post-2022 employment evidence for developing countries at all;
its Winkler background papers are exposure and task-content cross-sections
on the same ILO index and GLD collection this panel uses, so a quarterly
employment follow-up is the missing piece they point to. Ravanilla uses one
country with observed-usage exposure; Aguirre and Meloni use one country
with potential exposure. This panel has nine countries on a common schema,
the ILO potential score with quintile and continuous variants, the
new-hire and under-25 margins, and the call-centre cell identified in seven
countries. The natural contributions are the cross-country comparison and
the call-centre case; the natural additions from the two papers are the
Anthropic-usage exposure (Ravanilla) and wages plus formality as outcomes
(Aguirre and Meloni), both of which the schema already carries.
